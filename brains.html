How brains evolved across ten dimensions

No single number captures a brain. If head weight decided intelligence, the 7.8-kilogram sperm whale would rule Earth; if raw neuron count decided it, the 257-billion-neuron African elephant would beat us three to one; if packing density decided it, a twelve-gram songbird crams four times more neurons per gram than we do. Tracking 42 landmark stages across ten physical and architectural dimensions—from spare synapse proteins in single-celled cousins to 20-watt human language brains and 120-megawatt silicon clusters—reveals why every mind is a different compromise with physics.

04 · Six surprises in the data

Why bigger isn’t always smarter—and where biology still beats silicon

Paradox 1 · Elephant vs. Human

257 billion neurons—and 97.5% sit in the cerebellum

For decades textbooks guessed human brains held 100 billion neurons and larger elephant brains must hold even more cortical machinery. Isotropic fractionator cell counts revealed the twist: an African elephant holds 257 billion total neurons, but 251 billion pack into its cerebellum to coordinate a 40,000-muscle sensorimotor trunk, leaving 5.6 billion in its cerebral cortex versus 16.3 billion in ours.

Human cortex: 16.3B | Elephant cortex: 5.6B | Elephant cerebellum: 251B
Paradox 2 · Raven vs. Monkey

Monkey-scale forebrains inside a 14.5-gram walnut

Because flying animals pay a brutal weight penalty for every extra gram in the skull, songbirds and parrots ditched heavy six-layer cortical sheets for ultra-compact nuclear clusters that pack up to 247 million neurons per gram and burn roughly one-third the glucose per neuron of mammalian cortex—letting a raven hold 1.2 billion forebrain neurons, ahead of a capuchin monkey.

Raven: 14.5 g → 1.20B pallial neurons | Capuchin: 52 g → 1.14B
Paradox 3 · Metabolic Champions

A fish at 60% oxygen share—and human 5-year-olds at 52%

Adult humans spend ~20% of resting energy on a brain that weighs 2% of the body, yet two stages go far beyond that: Peters’ elephantnose fish devotes 60% of whole-body oxygen to a giant cerebellum decoding self-made electric fields, while a four-to-five-year-old human child temporarily wires ~300 trillion synapses, burning over half the resting body’s glucose so body growth stalls while grammar locks in.

Adult human: 20% | 5-yr human child: ~52% | Gnathonemus fish: 60%
Paradox 4 · The Cable Bottleneck

Without myelin, human spinal cords would have to be tree trunks

In bare unmyelinated nerve fibers, spike velocity grows only with the square root of fiber diameter: to hit 25 m/s, a squid must fuse hundreds of cells into a 1-millimeter giant axon. Jawed vertebrates wrapped axons in insulating myelin so impulses leap node-to-node at 80–120 m/s inside 10-micrometer fibers at 1% of the ATP cost per centimeter.

Squid 1,000 µm bare: 25 m/s | Mammal 10 µm myelinated: 100 m/s
Paradox 5 · Universal 39 bits/s

Fast-syllable Japanese and dense Mandarin carry the exact same bit rate

Across 17 spoken languages spanning nine language families, syllable rates vary nearly twofold (from ~5 syllables/s in Mandarin and Thai to ~8 in Japanese and Spanish), yet bit-per-syllable density trades off inversely so every human spoken stream converges near 39.1 bits per second—suggesting a shared auditory-prefrontal bottleneck along the arcuate loop.

Spoken rate ≈ 39.1 bits/s across 17 languages (Coupé et al. 2019)
Paradox 6 · Moravec’s Inversion

Silicon started at Breakthrough 5 and is working backward

Vertebrates spent 500 million years stacking 3D steering, dopamine reward, hippocampal simulation and social mind-reading before adding recursive language in the last ~100,000 years. Frontier AI clusters trained straight on humanity’s written archive—mastering 200 languages and formal proofs at 120 megawatts while still struggling with a toddler’s 20-watt sensorimotor dexterity.

Human skull: 20 W · 7.5×10¹² ops/J | 100k-GPU AI: 120 MW · 1.7×10¹² ops/J
