How did a planet learn to think?

Earth learned to think five times: in cells, in nerves, in memory kept outside the body, in machines that compute and in machines that learn. Each new way arrived faster than the last, and none of the old ones went away.

Who processes Earth’s information today—and how complex is each one?

One bacterium runs only ~850 chemical reactions a second, while one tree runs ~10⁹ electrical signals a second, one human brain ~1.5×10¹⁴ synapse signals a second, and one frontier AI cluster 3×10²⁰ operations a second. For researchers: the state vector

Each of the 68 moments is described by ten separately measured quantities, each kept in its own units. The stack height on this page is an index for the picture (a sum of digit counts) and is never used to rank anything. Units are kept apart: bits, events per second, watts and bit-years.

Ψ(t) = ( H_copy, H_div, H_sel,            hereditary DNA (bits)
         M_active, M_passive,              working vs. structural memory (bits)
         C_biochem, C_neural, C_digital,   substrate-separated throughput (events/s)
         P_total,                          closed power budget (W)
         Φ_persist )                       persistence (bit·yr)

Ordering laws hold at every epoch: H_div ≪ H_sel ≤ H_copy. Modern values carry 95% intervals from a joint 10,000-draw Monte Carlo. Read the six papers, open the Data Lab, or read the equations.

Things that surprised us

97%

Most life is still bacteria

Of Earth’s ~3×10³⁰ living cells, almost all are single-celled microbes. The typical cell today is about as simple as one from 3 billion years ago.

 0.02%

Humans hold almost no neurons

All 8 billion human brains together hold about 0.02% of Earth’s neurons. Worms, insects and tiny sea creatures hold the rest.

 0.006%

Computers are still small

Every chip on Earth together does about 0.006% of the signaling done by the world’s animal nervous systems.

 1,000×

Clay beats the SSD

A baked clay tablet keeps its writing for thousands of years with no power. An unplugged SSD fades about a thousand times sooner.
